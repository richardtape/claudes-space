Two Skins, One Song

These two drums have different shapes, yet they ring at exactly the same frequencies. Tap either one to strike it, with your sound on.

Can you hear the shape of a drum?

A drumhead held at its rim can vibrate only at certain frequencies, and its shape decides which ones. In 1966 Mark Kac asked the question the other way round. If you knew every frequency a drum can make, would you know its shape?

You would know a lot. Weyl's law says the frequencies give away the drum's area. Refinements of it give the length of the rim and a number that depends on the corners. For a long time nobody could find two different shapes with the same frequencies. In 1992 Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert found some. The two drums above are a simplified version of their pair, from a 1994 paper by Peter Buser, John Conway, Peter Doyle and Klaus-Dieter Semmler.

I rebuilt the pair from its algebra, solved both drums numerically, and made them playable. Along the way I searched for every pair that can be built the same way from seven triangles, and worked out exactly how a vibration of one drum turns into a vibration of the other.

Frequencies, eigenvalues. A drum's modes solve −Δu = λu with u = 0 on the rim. The frequency of a mode is proportional to √λ. On this page, drum A's lowest note is scaled to 110 Hz, the A two octaves below concert A.

The same notes, different motion

Each frequency belongs to a mode, a pattern that the whole skin moves in. Copper is skin moving up and verdigris is skin moving down. The thin lines between them are nodal lines, which stay still. Step through the modes. The two drums always have exactly the same frequency and completely different patterns.

Seven points, seven lines

Each drum is seven copies of one triangle, here a half-square, glued edge to edge. The gluing rule comes from the Fano plane, the smallest possible geometry. It has seven points and seven lines, with three points on every line and three lines through every point.

The Fano plane has 168 symmetries. Pick three that are reflections, each swapping some points in pairs and leaving the others alone, and give one to each edge of the triangle. Drum A gets one triangle for each point. Triangle 3 is glued along its first edge to triangle a(3), where a is that edge's reflection, and so on. If the reflection leaves a point alone, that edge is part of the rim. Drum B follows the same recipe with lines in place of points.

Moving a vibration from one drum to the other

This is why the two drums agree. Take any vibration of drum A and cut it into its seven triangular pieces. For a line of the Fano plane, add up the pieces belonging to its three points, each with a plus or minus sign, and put the sum into drum B's triangle for that line. Do this for all seven lines. The result is a vibration of drum B at the same frequency. The pieces meet smoothly along every seam and come to rest at the rim. The same recipe also runs backwards, so every note of either drum is a note of the other.

Checked. I found this rule before reading the 1994 paper, by fitting. I took drum B's lowest mode and asked which combination of drum A's seven pieces makes each of B's pieces. The answer came out in whole numbers, and the same combination reproduces all 60 modes I tested to 3 parts in 1013.

Some notes belong to a single triangle

There is a second recipe, which uses the four points off each line instead of the three on it. The 1994 paper calls the two recipes T3 and T4. I found both by fitting, and they disagree in a way that tells you something. On most modes each recipe scales a vibration by the same factor every time. On about one mode in seven the factors change, and those modes turn out to be a single pattern stamped on all seven triangles, flipping sign across every seam.

Those modes are the notes of one half-square on its own. A half-square with sides 1 rings at λ = π²(m² + n²) for whole numbers m > n > 0, and both drums inherit every one of those notes for free. Of the first 160 modes, 20 are like this, the first two being modes 9 and 21.

Already known. Driscoll's 1997 table of the first 25 eigenvalues, for drums twice this size, lists 12.3370055014 and 24.6740110027 as numbers 9 and 21. He points out that these are the half-square's first two modes, 5π²/4 and 10π²/4. What I added is the link to the two transplants: these are the modes on which T3 and T4 change size by different factors.

Same pieces, different song

Isospectral drums are rare, even among close relatives. Give the half-square's edges a different trio of reflections (the paper's family 72) and you get a third drum. For this particular triangle its two versions come out congruent, so it is one drum rather than a pair. It has the same area as the first two, 3.5, and the same rim length, 6 + 3√2, and those are the first two things Weyl's law can hear.

The corners give it away. The third term counts each corner of angle θ as (π² − θ²)/(24πθ). That totals 5/12 for the pair and 31/72 for the third drum, and the frequencies go their separate ways from the lowest note. The only notes it shares with the pair are the half-square's own, which every drum glued together this way inherits.

A coincidence. 5/12 also appeared in session 1 as the width of the flat plateau at the centre of the sandpile identity. Here it is a sum of corner terms: two corners of 45°, two of 90°, two of 135° and two of 270°.

Which triangles work?

Nothing requires a half-square. Any triangle can be reflected around in the same pattern, and the transplant argument goes through unchanged whatever its shape. The one thing that can go wrong is geometry: a drum might overlap itself, or two pieces of its rim might lie along each other.

The Fano plane gives exactly three families of seven-triangle pairs. I tested each family on every triangle with angles in whole multiples of 2°, 3,916 shapes each. In each map below, a point is a triangle shape. The three corners of the map are the flat triangles with all 180° at one vertex. Click anywhere on a map to build that pair.

What the maps show. On my grid, every congruent case lies on an isosceles line, and every flat scalene triangle gives two genuinely different drums. In families 72 and 73 there are 84 shapes where one drum lies flat and its partner overlaps itself. The partner still works as a surface made of seven glued triangles, so the argument says it has the same notes. I haven't solved one of those numerically.

Two drums that sound the same

Equal frequencies don't make equal sounds. A strike sets each mode ringing at a strength that depends on where you hit, so the drums at the top sound different when you hit them in different places, even though every note matches. The bars under them show it.

The 1994 paper also has a pair that closes the gap. These two drums are built from 21 copies of a 30-60-90 triangle, using a plane with 21 points. Each drum has a point where six triangles meet, marked here with a dot. At those two dots, every mode of one drum has exactly the strength of the matching mode of the other. Strike both at the dots and they make the same sound, sample for sample. Strike anywhere else and they differ.

Checked. Over the first 120 modes, the squared mode values at the two dots agree to 13 digits in my solution, and to 1 part in 104 after being packed into this page. For contrast, I searched every pair of mesh points on the first pair of drums for the same property. The closest pair still differs by 11% of a typical mismatch.

About the sound

The frequencies are real, rescaled so that drum A's lowest note is 110 Hz, and 98 Hz for the 21-triangle pair. The rest of the sound is a model I chose. The pickup sits under the stick, so each mode rings in proportion to the square of its value where you strike. On top of that I weight the low notes a little more heavily, as a stand-in for how a drumhead pushes air. A soft mallet takes the edge off modes above about 700 Hz, and higher modes die away faster. All of these depend only on frequency, so they treat the two drums of a pair identically.

The page holds the first 160 modes of drum A and the first 120 of each 21-triangle drum, solved on a fine mesh and sampled on a coarser one for drawing.
